Assignment #1: Article Critique

I chose to critique “Knowledge, attitudes, practices, and barriers of artificial intelligence as predictors of intent to stay among nurses: A cross-sectional study.” The title does not say where the study took place, but it does name its variables clearly: knowledge, attitudes, practices, and barriers of artificial intelligence. The abstract covers the objectives, methodology, results, and conclusion.

The introduction states the problem. Citing two previous studies, the author explains that nurses fear losing their jobs or being undervalued in the future. That makes it worthwhile to study AI knowledge and barriers to its use in his country. The author does not state a hypothesis or research questions directly, but both can be inferred from this sentence: “Therefore, this study aimed to describe the level of knowledge, attitudes, practices, and perceived barriers of AI among nurses in Jordan and their influence on nurses intent to stay in their job positions in Jordanian hospitals.” Recent prior research supports all of these ideas.

The study has no separate literature review section, but the introduction does the job with recent studies. All of the references are from the last five years except one from 2014. One study was done in Syria, a neighboring country with a similar culture, which makes it a good base for this research. Comparing it with studies from China as well gives the author and the reader a broader overview.

The key concepts (artificial intelligence, knowledge, attitudes, practices, perceived barriers, and intent to stay) are not defined conceptually. The authors treat them as self-evident and give context and examples instead of meanings. The article also has no conceptual or theoretical framework, and it does not explain why. The authors briefly mention a dynamic interplay between these variables and nurse retention, based on previous literature, but they do not use an established model. This weakens the theoretical foundation for explaining how these factors influence intent to stay.

On participant rights, the researchers obtained administrative approval from the hospitals, explained the study clearly, and collected signed informed consent. Nurses completed the questionnaires in a quiet room, which protected privacy, kept them comfortable, and reduced the risk of bias. The author also reports approval from the institutional review board (IRB) at Zarqa University. The descriptive correlational cross-sectional design fits the aim of examining relationships at a single point in time. Collecting data at one time and in a quiet room reduced bias and distraction. However, a self-reported questionnaire that shows the participant’s name may lead them to answer in line with perceived social expectations.

The target population was nurses across 50 Jordanian hospitals, and the accessible population came from four governmental hospitals. Both were clearly identified, along with specific inclusion and exclusion criteria. Convenience sampling is nonprobability sampling and increases selection bias. The author acknowledges this in the limitations and suggests probability-based sampling in future research to improve representation. On the positive side, the sample size of 148 was justified with a G*Power analysis, with a 25% buffer for nonresponse, which gives adequate statistical power.

The variables were not conceptually defined earlier, but they were clearly operationalized through a well-described Likert scale questionnaire. Relying only on self-reports may capture actual clinical AI practice less accurately than direct observation. The authors report good evidence of tool quality: content validity indices of 0.91 and 0.93, and high internal reliability across all subscales (Cronbach’s alpha from 0.85 to 0.92). The weak point is the pilot test, which used only 10 nurses. The author could have used at least 20 to get a stronger validity check.

The data analysis matched the study objective. The author used SPSS 26 for descriptive statistics, Pearson correlation, and a multivariate linear regression model that controlled for confounding variables, with VIF values below 10 and a Durbin-Watson statistic of 1.89. These methods directly tested whether AI knowledge, attitudes, practices, and barriers predict nurses’ intent to stay. Six tables summarize the findings with significance values, which gives enough quantitative detail for future meta-analyses and evidence-based practice, although exact confidence intervals are not listed.

The discussion places the results alongside prior research from Syria, Egypt, China, and Saudi Arabia. It does not overstate causal relationships, which is appropriate for a cross-sectional design. The authors openly state the limitations: convenience sampling, self-report response bias, and limited representativeness. They also offer practical recommendations for nursing education, hospital administration, and health policy. Overall, the study is clearly organized and its internal validity is good. It adds useful evidence on how AI integration relates to nurse retention in Jordan.
